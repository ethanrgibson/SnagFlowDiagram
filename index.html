Patient Entries / Incoming texts / how it works now, and where it is going

How Snag handles an incoming text

Two pictures of the same moment. First, what the app does today when a client texts in — sorted by the kind of message they sent. Then, the same moment once Snag reads every message first and decides what it is.

What these diagrams assume

The client is already enrolled and has agreed to receive texts, and their message reaches us. Neither of those is interesting to look at: if a message never arrives, that is a fault to fix, not a path to design. So every route here is the route that works.

Reading the diagrams

Colour tells you who did it

Amber means Snag was involved. Everything else the app did on its own. Across the two diagrams, the amount of amber and where it sits is the entire story.

Diagram 1 · today

A client texts in

Read each row left to right: the kind of message, what the app does about it, and what the client gets back. Where Snag is involved, its box holds the question it was asked, and the arrows leaving that box show what each answer leads to — including what happens when it says no, or cannot tell.

The app works out which row it is on by looking for keywords. Snag is only ever brought in after a keyword has already been spotted, to check the guess or to read a detail out of the message.

Every Snag box here is dashed, because none of them run every time — each has a condition, written inside the box. Two of them are switched off entirely for clinics that have AI turned off. Most messages that reach us today are never seen by Snag at all.

Every one of these is also saved. Whatever else a message turns out to be, the client's words are kept for the provider to read — asking for an appointment does not stop it being a journal entry too. And every no lands somewhere safe: when Snag says no or cannot tell, nothing is invented — the message falls back to an ordinary entry, or nothing changes at all.

What the client actually receives

The exact wording, as sent today. Every reply that could be mistaken for medical advice carries the emergency line.

Asking for an appointment Thanks — we've shared your request with your provider's office, and they'll check the schedule and get back to you about times. If this is an emergency, please call 911.
Cancelling an appointment Thanks — we've let your provider's office know and they'll follow up with you directly to confirm. If this is an emergency, please call 911.
Asking for a reminder You'll get a reminder every day at 8:00pm. Reply "Reminder off" to turn them off or "Reminder list" to see active reminders.
Turning a reminder off Turned off your 8:00pm reminder. Text a time like "Reminder 8:00pm" to set a new one.

Opt-out keyword You have unsubscribed and can no longer send journal entries. Re-enroll any time at patiententries.com/enroll
The one thing worth knowing about today's version

Row seven wins over everything else. While a conversation with the provider is open, every message the client sends is treated as conversation — so if they say “actually, can we push it back a day?” in the middle of one, it gets passed along as words and is never logged as a scheduling request. The app only ever lets a message be one thing at a time. That is the limitation the next diagram removes.

Diagram 2 · with triage

Snag reads it first

Same moment, different order. Instead of the app hunting for keywords and calling Snag to check its guess, Snag reads every incoming message once and answers two questions about it. Everything the app does next follows from those two answers.

The two questions are deliberately kept apart, because a message is often both things at once. Asking them separately is what lets a message be passed to the provider and logged as a scheduling request in the same breath — the thing today's version cannot do.

Note the Snag box below is solid, not dashed. That is the whole change in one detail: in the diagrams above, every Snag box carried a condition and most messages never reached one. Here there is no condition to carry.

How well it reads them, and what it costs

Measured over 542 real and hand-written message shapes, each run three times, against gemma-4-26b-a4b — the strongest of the seven models we tested. Every figure below belongs to that one model. None of them is an average across the field.

What the two accuracy figures actually measure. Snag answers two questions about every message — what are they asking for? and is this part of a conversation already in progress? — so there are two ways to be right.

92.9% is the strict one: both answers correct on the same message. Getting either half wrong sends that message down the wrong path, so this is the number a client would actually feel.

98.9% is only the second question, scored on its own: is this message outside any conversation, continuing one, or wrapping one up. The first question — working out what the client wants — is the harder half at 93.2%, and that is where nearly all the remaining mistakes sit.

Why those two costs look so far apart: one text is not one token. Reading a single message costs about 1,450 tokens — roughly 1,420 of them are the instructions and examples we send with every message, and only about 30 are Snag's answer coming back. So a message costs about a fifth of a cent, and the bill is almost entirely for explaining the job rather than for the reply.

If Snag gets one wrong

When Snag cannot make sense of a message, it says so, and the app saves it as an everyday journal entry — exactly what happens today to anything the keywords do not recognise. So a bad read costs a missed opportunity, never a lost message. That is the same floor we already live with, which is what makes this safe to turn on.

The difference

Three things actually change

Who decides what a message is
Today
The app looks for keywords and Snag confirms the guess. Anything phrased outside the expected wording is quietly filed as an everyday entry.
With triage
Snag decides, once, on every message. How well it reads people stops being a guessing game and becomes a number we can watch.
Messages sent mid-conversation
Today
The conversation wins. A request to move an appointment made mid-chat is passed along as words and never logged, so it never reaches the schedule.
With triage
Both things happen. The provider still sees the message, and the request is logged at the same time.
Ending a conversation
Today
Snag watches the back-and-forth and nudges the provider once to close it. The provider still has to remember to do it.
With triage
A wrap-up is recognised as it arrives, so the conversation can close itself. No nudge, nothing for the provider to remember.

What does not change: enrolment, consent, and the opt-out and help keywords. Those stay mechanical and word-for-word, because the carriers require them to be. Snag never gets a say in whether someone has opted out.
